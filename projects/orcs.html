What was I working on?
// UX DESIGNER

A VR action-adventure title within the Orcs Must Die franchise, reimagined with a strong focus on melee combat rather than traditional tower defense.

As the UX Designer for the project, I led the design of core interaction systems, including object handling, inventory, weapon upgrades, and armory flows, as well as the full FTUX. I also developed the combat language — defining clear visual and systemic signifiers to guide players on when to attack, block, or parry — ensuring combat felt intuitive, responsive, and satisfying within VR.

COMBAT LANGUAGE SYSTEM DESIGN PROCESS
WHAT WAS THE CHALLENGE?
[ ORCS — CHALLENGE IMAGE ]

In a VR melee combat game, players need to understand moment-to-moment when to attack, when to block, and when to parry — without relying on traditional UI overlays that would break immersion.

The challenge was to build a combat language entirely through spatial, visual, and systemic cues, making the system feel readable and intuitive to new players while remaining deep and responsive for experienced ones.

ATTACK SIGNIFIERS
[ ORCS IMAGE 01 ]
Visual signifiers were designed to clearly indicate enemy vulnerability windows, guiding players toward the right moment to strike.
The system relied on in-world cues rather than HUD overlays, keeping players immersed while still communicating clearly.
Iterations focused on balancing readability with aesthetic coherence — signifiers had to feel part of the world, not layered on top of it.
Playtesting revealed that timing feedback was critical — players needed to feel the window, not just see it.

FTUX FLOW
[ ORCS IMAGE 04 ]
The FTUX was designed to introduce the combat language progressively, layering mechanics one at a time without breaking the player's sense of agency.
Each tutorial beat was contextual — teaching through combat encounters rather than static instruction screens.
Pacing was carefully calibrated so players felt competent before each new mechanic was introduced, reducing frustration during early sessions.
Playtest data directly shaped the sequence, reordering beats based on where players struggled or disengaged.

LEARNINGS
[ IMAGE PLACEHOLDER ]
Combat readability is a spatial problem
In VR, information can't rely on flat-screen conventions. Designing a combat language meant rethinking how visual, audio, and haptic signals work together in three-dimensional space.
Trust the player to learn through doing
The most effective FTUX beats were the ones that put mechanics in front of players without explicit instruction — letting the system speak for itself through well-designed encounters.
